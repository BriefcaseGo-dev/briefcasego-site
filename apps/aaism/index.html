Study Guide

Tap any section to expand

The AAISM™ exam tests your understanding of fundamental AI and machine learning concepts. Master the terminology in this section — these definitions appear throughout all three domains.

ML Paradigms
  • Supervised learning — Uses labeled data to predict known targets (classification and regression).
  • Unsupervised learning — Finds patterns with no labels (clustering, anomaly detection).
  • Reinforcement learning — Agent learns by trial-and-error with rewards in an environment.

Overfitting vs. Underfitting — Overfitting: model memorizes training data, fails on new data. Underfitting: model too simple, poor on both. Know these cold for the exam.

Exam Weight

Covers stakeholder considerations, AI strategies, policies, asset/data lifecycle, security program development, and business continuity.

Key AI Governance Components
  • Mature IT governance processes covering stakeholder needs and compliance monitoring
  • Appropriate governance structures: board of directors, IT strategy committee, risk/audit committees
  • The governing body is ultimately responsible and accountable — this cannot be delegated
EU AI Act Risk Tiers
  • Unacceptable Risk — Prohibited (social credit scoring, behavioral manipulation)
  • High Risk — Strict oversight required (healthcare, law enforcement)
  • Limited Risk — Transparency obligations (chatbots must disclose they are AI)
  • Minimal Risk — No specific obligations (spam filters)
Exam Weight

Covers AI risk frameworks, threat modeling, OWASP LLM Top 10, and vendor/supply chain management.

NIST AI RMF — Four Core Functions
  • GOVERN — Establishes risk policies and accountability structures
  • MAP — Identifies AI system characteristics and risk areas
  • MEASURE — Analyzes, assesses, and benchmarks AI risks
  • MANAGE — Prioritizes and addresses identified risks; responds and recovers

Glossary

157 key terms — each with an expandable real-world example
AI Management System (AIMS)
D1

A systematic framework of policies, objectives, and processes an organization establishes to direct and control its AI-related activities — the ISO/IEC 42001 concept underlying certification.

A company scales from one pilot AI project to a dozen production systems without ever formalizing an AI management system — each team defines its own ad hoc rules for data handling, model review, and incident response. When a regulator asks for evidence of consistent AI governance, there's no single framework to point to, only a patchwork of team-specific practices. The company's ISO 42001 certification effort starts from scratch, building the AIMS retroactively instead of having grown it alongside the AI program.

General-Purpose AI (GPAI)
D2

Under the EU AI Act, an AI model or system displaying significant generality and capable of a wide range of tasks regardless of market placement — includes foundation models, subject to distinct obligations from narrower, single-purpose systems.

A company building a narrow fraud-detection tool assumes GPAI obligations don't apply, and they're right. But when they later license a foundation model for an open-ended internal chatbot, GPAI obligations — model evaluation, incident tracking, cybersecurity — apply in a way their narrow tool never triggered, because generality itself determines this category, not risk level.

AI Governance
D1

The senior oversight, processes, standards, and safeguards ensuring safe and ethical use of AI — addressing inherent flaws from human-created AI including bias and discrimination. The governing body bears ultimate, undelegable accountability for all AI activities.

A logistics company's routing AI starts systematically deprioritizing deliveries to a lower-income zip code because of biased historical data. Engineers notice the pattern, but no one has authority to halt the system — governance was never formalized beyond "the data science team handles it." When a complaint escalates to a lawsuit, the board — who holds ultimate, undelegable accountability regardless of who built the model — has no oversight structure to show regulators.

Data Poisoning
D2

Manipulation of training or validation data to introduce vulnerabilities, backdoors, or biases into the resulting model — a primary supply chain risk for AI systems.

A competitor secretly contributes manipulated samples to a public dataset that a company later uses to fine-tune its customer-service model. Months later, the model responds strangely to a specific, obscure phrase — a backdoor the poisoned samples planted — letting the competitor trigger embarrassing outputs on demand. Because nobody validated the training data's provenance before fine-tuning, the sabotage went undetected until it was already deployed.

Human-in-the-Loop (HITL)
D1

A qualified human overseeing AI outputs and making final decisions, particularly for high-stakes applications. Required for high-risk AI under the EU AI Act. HITL must involve genuine independent judgment, not rubber-stamping.

A bank's AI-driven loan-denial system has a "human review" step, but reviewing officers are told to approve the AI's call in over 95% of cases and are evaluated on throughput, not accuracy. When a discrimination complaint is investigated, regulators determine the review was rubber-stamping, not genuine independent judgment — meaning the EU AI Act's HITL requirement was violated in substance even with a human technically "in the loop."

Explainability
D3

A representation of the mechanisms underlying an AI system's operation — the "how" — distinct from Interpretability, which concerns the meaning of an output (the "why").

A bank can describe exactly which layers and weights activated to produce a loan denial — genuine explainability — but the loan officer still can't tell the applicant anything meaningful about why their situation led to that outcome. Explainability without an interpretability layer leaves perfect technical detail and no usable answer.

Prompt Injection
D2

An attack where crafted inputs override an LLM's system instructions, causing it to ignore safety guidelines, reveal confidential information, or take harmful actions.

A company's customer-facing chatbot processes documents uploaded by users, and an attacker embeds hidden instructions inside a PDF telling the model to ignore its safety guidelines and reveal internal system prompts. Because the malicious instructions arrived inside processed content rather than the visible chat message, this is indirect prompt injection — easy to miss if a team only screens direct user input.

Model Drift
D3

Gradual degradation of ML model performance due to changes in data distributions or relationships between inputs and outputs over time. Two types: data drift and concept drift.

A demand-forecasting model performs reliably for two years, then starts over-predicting after a competitor shifts customer behavior. Investigation determines this is concept drift, not data drift — the inputs look statistically similar, but the input-output relationship changed. The distinction matters because the fix differs from what a data-drift diagnosis would prescribe.
