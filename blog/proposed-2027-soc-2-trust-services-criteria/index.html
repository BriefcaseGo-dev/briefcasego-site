Proposed 2027 SOC 2 Criteria: What Changes, Where AI Fits

November 2026 · 5 min read

On October 28 the AICPA released an exposure draft of new SOC 2 Trust Services Criteria. It is the first restructuring of the criteria since 2017, because the 2022 update revised only the points of focus. Nothing changes for audits now underway. If the draft is approved, it would apply to engagements beginning on or after December 31, 2027, and service organizations could adopt it early. Public comments are open until January 15, 2027. That gives service organizations about a year to prepare, and anyone who relies on SOC 2 reports a short window to influence the result.

What the Draft Changes

The draft itself sits behind a free AICPA login, and the AICPA's announcement describes it only in general terms. The structural details below come from reporting by Accounting Today, which interviewed practitioners who worked on the draft, and from a commentary by the president of a large SOC 2 firm. The two accounts agree.

  • Fewer common criteria: the common criteria drop from 33 to 20. Overlapping concepts are merged and sections reordered. A smaller count doesn't mean less testing, since much of the content moves rather than disappears.
  • No COSO tie-in: in the 2017 criteria, CC1 through CC5 are built directly on COSO's 17 internal control principles. The draft removes that link. A service organization would instead decide up front which internal control framework it follows, such as COSO or a NIST framework.
  • Governance language: references to the board of directors give way to those charged with governance, with more emphasis on oversight of specific systems than on corporate boards.
  • Risk assessment in one place: risk assessment content that is currently spread across several criteria is pulled into a single section that runs from setting up the program through operating and monitoring it.
  • Change management split three ways: today, CC8.1 covers every change in one criterion. The draft separates infrastructure changes, acquired software and internally developed software.

Where AI Fits

The AICPA's own announcement lists the new AI-related points of focus: AI governance, model monitoring, prompt manipulation, data poisoning, transparency of AI-generated outputs, training data management, and the risks of automated decision-making. Under the 2017 criteria and the 2022 points of focus, AI isn't mentioned at all.

There is no standalone AI criterion. According to the reporting, the working group spread AI points of focus across existing criteria, on the reasoning that AI touches too many areas to isolate and doesn't change the underlying need for controls. That is a sound design choice, but it has a practical side effect. Points of focus are guidance, not requirements, and the AICPA says plainly that not all of them must be addressed. Whether a given report actually tests model monitoring or prompt manipulation will depend on how the service organization defines its system and how its auditor scopes the work.

Where this crosses into AI governance: This is an IT audit question, but it overlaps with Domain 2 of ISACA's published AAISM™ exam outline (AI Risk Management). A SOC 2 report is usually the main assurance a customer gets from an AI vendor, so what the criteria cover sets the limits of third-party AI due diligence.

What to Do Before It's Final

The draft can still change, so this isn't the time to rebuild a control matrix. A few things are worth starting now, though.

  • Run a dry mapping: if you own a SOC 2 program, map your current controls against the redline. The consolidation moves content between criteria, and early adopters will want to know where each control lands.
  • Settle the framework question: if your control environment and risk assessment narratives lean on the COSO mapping, decide whether you'll keep COSO or adopt another framework, and document why.
  • Separate your change populations: inventory acquired software and internally developed software as distinct flows. If coding agents write part of your code, the internally developed population is where that evidence will sit.
  • Ask your AI vendors about AI scope: a clean SOC 2 under the 2017 criteria says nothing direct about model behavior. Ask whether they plan to adopt early and whether the AI points of focus will be in scope. Until then, look for separate evidence such as an AI-specific certification or independent testing.
  • Comment if you rely on these reports: user entities, not just audit firms, can tell the AICPA whether points of focus alone are enough to cover AI risk.
